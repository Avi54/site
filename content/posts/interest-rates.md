In a September 2026 post, Aswath Damodaran revisits how higher interest rates affect stock prices. He writes in the context of long-term Treasury yields at levels not seen in twenty years. The 10-year moved from $4.18\%$ at the start of 2026 to $4.75\%$ by the end of August. However, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both rose strongly over the same eight months. Two ideas from his post are summarized here to explain how both can hold at once.

## Differences between Stocks and Bonds

The effect of higher rates on bonds is direct. The coupon is fixed when the bond is issued, so a higher discount rate lowers the present value and nothing beyond that changes.

A stock is different because its cash flows are what the business has left after running its operations, and those move with the economy that rates respond to. He identifies three channels. The first is revenue, which depends on why rates rose (inflation or higher real rates) and on how much pricing power the firm has to pass inflation on. The second is earnings, through the effect of rates on input costs, operating costs and interest expense. The third is reinvestment. When real rates rise, fewer projects clear the required return, so firms invest less. That raises near-term cash flow at the expense of future growth.

A simple perpetuity-growth example illustrates this. 

$$PV = \frac{C}{r - g}$$
$$PV = \frac{C}{8 \% - 3 \%} = \frac{C}{5 \%}$$

where $C$ is next year's cash flow, $r$ is the discount rate, and $g$ is the growth rate.

With $r = 8 \%$ and $g = 3 \%$, value is 20 times cash flow. If real rates rise and $r$ goes to $9\%$ with no change in $g$, value falls to about $16.7$ times, roughly $17 \%$ lower. If the rise is inflation-driven and the firm passes it through fully, $r$ goes to $9 \%$ and $g$ goes to $4 \%$. The gap is still $5 \%$, so value is unchanged. The discount rate rose by the same amount in both cases, but one lowers value by about 17% and the other leaves it unchanged.

For this reason, Damodaran sorts companies into three groups. A company is hurt when the higher discount rate outweighs any change in its cash flows, so its value falls. It is unaffected when the two effects cancel out. It is helped when its cash flows rise by more than enough to offset the higher discount rate, so its value goes up. In the example above, the first firm is hurt and the second is unaffected. The market's overall reaction depends on which group dominates, and risk premiums also shift when rates move. The perpetuity-growth model is a simplification, since real firms' growth is not perfectly tied to inflation, but the direction of the argument holds.

## Daily Sensitivity Versus the Full Year

To test the effect directly, he looked at the 169 trading days from January to the end of August. The 10-year yield rose on 84, fell on 73, and was unchanged on 12. On average, the S&P 500 fell almost half a percent on days when the yield rose by more than 3 basis points, and rose about half a percent on days when it fell by more than that. So stocks clearly react to yields in the short run, at least for larger moves.

Over the full period, stocks still rose because expected earnings did. Analysts raised their S&P 500 earnings estimates for both 2026 and 2027 by more than $11\%$ during the first eight months. In discount-rate terms, higher rates weighed on stocks on the days yields rose, and rising cash flow estimates more than offset that over the full period.

There are two caveats worth considering. First, the daily comparison is descriptive. Yields rise on some days because of strong growth news and on others because of inflation news, and the post does not separate them, so the half-percent figures blend both. Second, the resilience is concentrated. The aggregate tech sector returned $25.22\%$, while the median tech company returned $7.75\%$.
