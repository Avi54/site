The Goldsmiths' Alchemy: The Origin of Paper Money

July 22, 2026 · 3 min

Where did paper money actually come from? In Princes of the Yen, Richard Werner answers this by tracing it to a simple receipt slip, a piece of paper a medieval goldsmith handed over for gold left in safekeeping. That receipt is principally an ancestor to the bank note, and closely tied to the origins of paper money in Europe.

From Storage Slip to Money#

Goldsmiths had the strongest vaults in town, hence people deposited their gold with them and in exchange were given a receipt as proof of the deposit. Carrying gold around town to settle payments was risky and cumbersome. As a result over time instead of withdrawing gold to make a payment, they just handed over the receipt itself, trusting that whoever received it could redeem it for gold. The receipt began circulating from hand to hand as if it were gold.

Goldsmiths met regularly as part of their trade guild and realized that because receipts were circulating as money, much of the actual gold in their vaults sat completely idle, unclaimed for long stretches at a time. So they began quietly lending out gold that belonged to their depositors, earning interest on it while the original depositor still held a receipt they believed was fully backed. If they helped each other cover the occasional unexpected withdrawal, the risk of getting caught out was low. Purchasing power had been created that hadn’t existed before, the same gold was now backing two claims at once.

Demand for loans stayed strong even once most of the vault’s gold was lent out. Rather than turn borrowers away, goldsmiths took the final step. They handed out brand-new receipts, with no gold behind them at all. Now the receipt (paper money) became fully detached from gold a receipt was no longer a claim on a specific deposit, but a freestanding piece of paper accepted as money purely because people trusted it. Thus, goldsmiths had learned to print money and effectively created money much like a modern central bank does.

From Goldsmith to Banker#

Goldsmiths soon gave up working with gold and jewels altogether. Charging interest on paper slips that cost nothing to produce was a far more lucrative business, and it is why they came to be known as bankers. A receipt for safekeeping had become a financial instrument in its own right. Paper money created and issued at the banker’s discretion rather than backed one-for-one by metal in a vault.

A lasting consequence of this was that the amount of money in circulation was no longer decided by how much gold existed, but by how many receipts a goldsmith was willing to issue. Whoever controlled the printing of receipts controlled the money supply. A role that would later pass from individual goldsmith-bankers to chartered banks, and eventually to central banks. It highlights how the entity that controls money supply wields incredibly power.

References#

Werner, Richard A. Princes of the Yen: Japan’s Central Bankers and the Transformation of the Economy. Chapter 4, “The Alchemy of Banking.” M.E. Sharpe, 2003.
