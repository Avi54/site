Firm Foundations and Castles in the Air

September 8, 2026 · 3 min

In the opening chapter of A Random Walk Down Wall Street, Malkiel classifies nearly every approach to stock valuation as falling under one of two competing theories: the firm-foundation theory and the castle-in-the-air theory. He treats the two as close to mutually exclusive, and argues that understanding the gap between them is a prerequisite for avoiding serious investing mistakes.

The Firm-Foundation Theory#

The firm-foundation theory holds that every stock has an intrinsic value, anchored to the underlying business, which a careful analyst can estimate from present conditions and future prospects. When the market price drifts away from that anchor, the theory says, it eventually snaps back, so the investor’s job is simply to compare price against calculated value and act on the gap. In modern terms, this is often referred to as reversion to the mean, and the intrinsic value is calculated by discounting future cash flows into present value. In other words, a dollar promised next year is worth less than a dollar today.

Malkiel traces the theory’s formal grounding to John Burr Williams. The discounting framework produced became the basis for Benjamin Graham and David Dodd’s Security Analysis, which converted a generation of Wall Street analysts to hunting for stocks priced below their calculated worth. Malkiel names Warren Buffett as the theory’s most successful practitioner. He also flags where the theory is undermined. Estimating a firm’s long-term growth rate, and how long that growth can be sustained, is itself a forecast dressed up as arithmetic and is sensitive to the growth assumption made by the analyst. The “firm” foundation, in other words, still rests on assumptions about the future.

The Castle-in-the-Air Theory#

The second theory, which Malkiel attributes to Keynes’s 1936 writing on markets, drops the search for intrinsic value altogether and focuses on psychology instead. Keynes’s claim was that most professional investors don’t spend their effort forecasting a company’s long-run earnings. They spend it trying to anticipate how the crowd’s sentiment will shift a little before everyone else notices. The investor who does this well isn’t valuing the asset, they’re timing the mood. This is often now referred to as momentum investing.

This involves guessing what the crowd expects the crowd’s average opinion to be. Applied to markets, a stock is worth whatever price you can convince the next buyer to pay for it, regardless of any calculation about the business underneath. Malkiel doesn’t shy from the less flattering name this goes by on the Street, the “greater fool” theory.

Why the Distinction Matters#

The two theories diverge about what gives an asset its price. Firm-foundation investing treats price as something that should converge on value, so mispricing is a temporary error the market will correct. Castle-in-the-air investing treats price as whatever collective belief can sustain, so there’s no “correct” price to converge toward, only a crowd’s expectations, which can run far ahead of any underlying business reality before they eventually give out.

These theories intersect with real consequences. Malkiel points to speculative episodes, Dutch tulip bulbs, English stock bubbles, and, closer to his own writing, the dot-com run-up as cases where the castle-in-the-air dynamic took over almost entirely, and where those unable to exit before the crowd’s mood turned lost heavily. He recognizes that crowds genuinely do behave this way, and mass psychology genuinely does move prices. However, he argues that trying to trade on it, anticipating a shift in crowd sentiment ahead of the crowd itself, is a far riskier game than the firm-foundation approach, precisely because there’s no anchor to fall back on when the guess is wrong.

References#

Malkiel, Burton G. A Random Walk Down Wall Street: The Time-Tested Strategy for Successful Investing. Chapter 1, “Firm Foundations and Castles in the Air.”
